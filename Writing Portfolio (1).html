Short storyThe Smartest Man I Ever Met Didn’t Have a DegreeA day in a mechanic’s shop turns into a lesson about process.

“The only person who is educated is the one who has learned how to learn and change.”

It’s one of those quotes people like to throw around to make themselves sound intelligent. Personally, I think a quote like that is best understood when it’s embodied by a person. The first person I ever saw embody it was Mr. Kali.

He lived just down the street from us. Slender frame, strong arms, dark skin, exactly what you’d expect of a mechanic. I never paid much attention to him whenever he worked on my car, but today felt different.

I walked into the shop as I always did, parked my car, and told him the problem. Normally, I would have gone off to run an errand or grab a meal while he worked. Instead, I found myself sitting patiently in his shop.

“Oga, you no dey go today?”

“I no dey busy today. I go just wait, make I siddon.”

And just like that, the conversation was set in motion.

His television suddenly let out a loud crackling sound. Then the screen went black.

I felt bad for him. Where was he going to find the money to repair a television in this economy? Times were already hard enough without appliances deciding to give up the ghost.

Before I had finished throwing him a pity party in my head, Mr. Kali simply walked over to the television, pressed one or two buttons, adjusted a loose wire, and the screen flickered back to life.

I blinked.

“Now where did you learn how to do that?”

He looked at me and smiled.

“I always tell you youngsters with your fancy suits and exaggerated English degrees, no be by mouth person suppose get doings. I get doings.”

I stared at him, half amused, half offended.

Not only had he just taken a jab at a paying customer, but he said it with the confidence of someone who genuinely believed he was right.

“Old man, no be wetin you dey talk. This generation has innovation, great achievements, and advanced science.”

“Then fix the TV,” he replied with a hearty laugh. “And your car while you’re at it.”

I opened my mouth to respond.

Nothing came out.

“You see,” he said, lowering the lens of his safety goggles, “anybody can learn to do anything, as long as they bother to understand the process of a thing.”

“Okay,” I said. “So you’ve read a few instruction manuals. Big deal.”

He shook his head.

“I didn’t say the process of that thing. I said the process of a thing.”

He paused, giving me a look that suggested I had already missed his point.

“A thing can be anything. Once you understand that every worthwhile thing has a process, that there will be obstacles, that progress is rarely a straight line—you can learn almost anything.”

I sat back.

“Go on.”

“You see, one thing I’ve always noticed about your generation is haste.”

He reached for a wrench lying on the table beside him before continuing.

“It’s wonderful that you can now do in a day what took us years to learn. But you didn’t just remove the time, you removed the process. The understanding. The essence.”

OpinionWhy Fresh Graduates Don’t Get HiredThe wrestle with HR may just be a fight against yourself.

With over 600,000 graduates, 90,000 open jobs, a selection and interview margin of 33 and 1 percent, all amplified by the sound of the ever increasing poverty rate, it is safe to say the employment line has now been turned into a game of survivors. What may come as a major shock is finding out that the wrestle with HR may just be a fight against yourself. How graduates have been trained to think, reason, adapt and solve stand as the biggest obstacle to that dream job. Let’s delve, shall we?

First up on the list is the Educational gap. In Nigeria, the surest path to success is la cram, la pour. When sleepless nights of studying, revision and tutorials fail you, the best way to help and or save yourself is to cram and pour, but what you may not know is this mechanism you developed in secondary school won’t get you anywhere with your job. Why? Because nobody is asking you to pour anything, they want to see you use those things you crammed and threw away to solve real life problems. So you’ve spent all this while wondering why you are not moving past a certain stage in the hiring process, the reason is simple, an interviewer isn’t looking for textbook answers, they’re looking for the translation of practiced skill into explanatory words.

Next up on the list is the fast paced culture we have around us today. Fast food, fast music and fast learning. Everyone wants results as quickly as possible, and that mindset has slowly changed the way we approach our craft. We consume information rapidly but rarely spend enough time mastering it.

Whether you’re auditioning for a play, applying for a graduate role at a company, or trying to build a career as a content creator, one thing remains true: if you don’t know the ABCs of your craft, you will always be booted out on the first phase of the selection process. It is important to take the time to become an expert at your craft, so when you apply for that job all you are saying is factually correct and a show of a concept you have internalized and let sit inside you, like a well brewed cup of tea.

Another challenge many graduates face today is the unrealistic picture of success created by social media.

Every day, we’re flooded with videos of people making huge amounts of money from seemingly simple tasks. After watching enough of them, it’s easy to believe that success should come quickly and effortlessly.

But that’s rarely how a career works.

Your first job may not pay an extraordinary salary. In fact, it probably won’t. What it should do is give you valuable experience, expose you to real-world challenges, and help you develop skills that increase your value over time.

When your only definition of success is earning six figures or making thousands of dollars immediately, you become vulnerable to scams, reject legitimate opportunities, and overlook experiences that could accelerate your growth in the long run.

Sometimes, the opportunity you’re turning down because it “doesn’t pay enough” is the very one that could open the doors you’re praying for five years from now.

It’s easy to blame impossible job requirements, unrealistic hiring criteria, or difficult HR managers, and to be fair, they deserve a share of the criticism.

But sometimes, the uncomfortable truth is that the work we’ve put into becoming employable simply isn’t enough.

The good news is that this isn’t a permanent problem.

You can fix it.

Master your craft one course at a time. Read one more book than everyone else. Build one more project. Practise one more skill. Learn to solve problems instead of memorizing answers.

The job market may be competitive, but competition is not the same as impossibility.

The graduates who continue learning long after graduation are often the ones employers eventually hire.

EssayHow Good and Pleasant: On Unity in WorshipWhat happens when very different people set themselves aside and praise together.

Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity! It is like the precious ointment upon the head, that ran down upon the beard, even Aaron’s beard: that went down to the skirts of his garments.

One of the things that make our praise beautiful to God is unity.

It is excellent to think that men and women so unalike in nature, physiology, colour, habits, behaviours, traits and patterns would come together to serve God.

A black man and a white woman, quite different, throw all that difference away when it is time to worship. In the house of God they become brother and sister, draping hands, aligning hearts, ridding themselves of themselves to pour glory to God.

In a regular situation they could have been fighting about anything or everything. But once the trumpets sound and it’s time to go into the house of the Lord, all of that changes. It changes more when the second horn is blown and they are before the Lord. When we say “Praise the Lord”, there is a great well that springs up in us coming from deep adoration and reverence for God.

What supplements that feeling is when you look beside you and you see someone with that same devotion. All bad moments taken to the side, you link your arms in theirs, connect your hearts and wells, and offer up water laced with adoration before the throne of the living God.

That sense of loyalty in unity is a very wonderful feat. To know that someone can feel the same way about your God. A pictorial evidence that God’s goodness is not fluky. I may praise because HE gave me a baby, she praises because HE healed her, we have both experienced God’s great grace in different ways. But what makes that more beautiful is how we remove self from the equation and place all focus on God, we are linking ourselves for the sole purpose of giving praise to God.

It is a very wonderful thing that we can come together as an ecclesia, individually different people, marked by blood, sending one signal to God.

I read once of this unity in a book by Kenneth Hagin. The people in his church were together just basking in quietness, offering heartfelt thanksgiving to God. They were there for who knows how long, then a thief came to rob the church, but the sheer power in their unified worship convicted him to surrender his life to Christ. That is how powerful unified praise is. No sermon was preached, no one raised an alarm, just that wonderful presence brought about the salvation of a man.

Indeed, how beautiful it is when brethren dwell in unity, it’s like that precious oil that was poured on the head of Aaron and flowed down to his garments.

Technical manual · excerptWhat Is PVT?From a manual I created, “How to Use PVTP”.

PVT stands for Pressure, Volume and Temperature. It refers to the laboratory analysis and experiments used to characterize reservoir fluids and to understand how their properties and phase behaviour change with pressure and temperature. PVT analysis helps us understand how the fluid will behave under reservoir and surface conditions as it is produced from the reservoir to the surface.

Why do we perform PVT analysis?

  • To understand how the reservoir fluid behaves and how its properties change under different pressure and temperature conditions.
  • To characterize the fluid and develop a reliable PVT model that can be used for material-balance calculations, reservoir simulation and dynamic modelling.
  • To provide the fluid-property and phase-behaviour information required for the design and operation of surface facilities, such as separators and other processing equipment.

Data obtained from PVT analysis

1. Compositional Analysis
Compositional analysis determines the chemical composition of the reservoir fluid by identifying the individual components and heavier hydrocarbon fractions present. It shows us how much of each pure hydrocarbon (C1, C2, C3), non-hydrocarbon (nitrogen, carbon dioxide) and pseudo-component is in the fluid. Pseudo-components are heavy fractions grouped together and written with a plus sign after them, e.g. C7+. The heavy fractions are characterized using properties such as molecular weight, density/specific gravity and other properties required for fluid modelling.

2. Constant Composition Expansion (CCE)
The Constant Composition Expansion experiment is used to study how fluid volume and phase behaviour change as pressure changes while the overall fluid composition stays constant.

No fluid is removed from the cell. For an oil system, as pressure is reduced below the bubble point, gas evolves from the oil, but the liberated gas stays inside the cell. Important information from this experiment includes the bubble-point pressure, relative volume and phase behaviour.

3. Differential Liberation Experiment (DLE)
The Differential Liberation Experiment is primarily used for reservoir oils. It is used to determine how the oil and the liberated gas behave as pressure is reduced below the bubble point.

At each pressure stage, the gas that evolves from the oil is removed from the cell, and the volume of the remaining oil is measured. Measurements from this experiment give us properties such as the oil formation volume factor (Bo), the solution gas-oil ratio (Rs), oil density and oil viscosity.

ExplainerStages of an Oil WellFrom exploration to plug and abandon, in eight stages.

1. Exploration: As the name implies, we are conducting an exploration to see how viable and fit the land is, we see major action from geologists and geophysicists, wildcat wells 🤪 are drilled to test the wells.

2. Site Preparation: Here we clear the land, create access roads, bring in equipment, essentially we are preparing the land for the operations ahead.

3. Drilling: (now the party is getting started 🥳) A hole is made into the ground to connect subsurface to the surface, steel pipes called casing are also inserted to provide structure and support, then it is cemented, then we insert our drilling mud to control pressure.

4. Well Completion: These are the finishing touches on our well, valves, tubing and perforation is done here, we also get to insert our Christmas tree 🥹🎄. At this stage, sometimes we can do acidizing, but our well is just about ready to start releasing the black gold.

5. Production 🤑: Oil and gas flow from the reservoir to the surface through the tubes we put in earlier, when the raw crude is collected, we separate it (water, gas, oil), it’s then sent through pipelines for the next stage.

6. Well Maintenance: At this stage damaged equipment is fixed, workover operations are performed, and blockages are cleared to ensure the smooth and continuous running of our well.

7. Enhanced Recovery: When the energy in the reservoir is no longer enough to push it to the surface, EOR is used to help: water, steam or gas is injected into the reservoir to assist the oil in coming out, it can increase our total recovery to about 70 percent.

8. Plug and Abandon: Finally, it’s time for the life of the well to come to an end, when the well is no longer commercially viable (it isn’t producing enough oil to make profit), we fill our well with cement, closing it down permanently 🥹💔, welp it had a good run.

LinkedIn series · part 1Crash Course in Pet EngMy five-year Petroleum Engineering journey, 100 and 200 level.

Hii, my name is Annie, and for the next two posts, I’m giving LinkedIn a crash course on my five-year Petroleum Engineering journey! While I can’t guarantee you a certification, I can assure you of a good read. Enjoy.

100 Level: The Principles of Science

Here we start off by learning the foundations of science (engineering and science students are usually mixed up). The core subjects? Physics, Math, Chemistry, and Introduction to Engineering Drawing (God save us all).

The point is to get acquainted with the basic principles that will be used later on in the engineering field. We learn stuff like: matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.

From that foundation, we explore the types of matter (solid, liquid, gas), their structure, and how various temperature, pressure, and external influences affect those states.

The Petroleum Connection: This foundation is crucial! It allows us to determine the best way to transport petroleum (liquid state) through pipelines and how to prevent costly issues like hydrates (a condition brought about by low temperature and high pressure). Understanding these states and their conditions is the first step toward effective innovation, like stopping gas flaring and utilizing or re-injecting the gas.

200 Level: Delving into Engineering Principles & The Practical Side 🛠️

We delve deeper into engineering principles, leaving the realm of scientific theories and looking at the more practical and design-conscious aspects.

Here, we get subjects like:

  • Fluid Mechanics: The response of fluids to forces. This shapes our understanding of how oil and gas flows through porous media. If you know Darcy’s Law, its key lingo is taught here first!
  • Thermodynamics: The relationship between heat, temperature, and energy.
  • Material Science: The study of the properties and composition of materials, plus more complex math (of course!).

When we learn Material Science, we learn about the various materials we can use to make equipment, why certain materials simply can’t be used, and the appropriate grades to select. We learn how materials interact with various forces and the best way of creating durable tools.

Example: Low carbon mild steel cannot be used for pipelines due to the corrosive components in the oil (CO2 & H2S). Instead, we learn that API 5L carbon steel grades are used due to their higher yield strengths, toughness, and ability to resist wear. We get this critical info directly from Material Science.
